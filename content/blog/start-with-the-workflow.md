It's easy to start a conversation about automation with a tool. A platform has a promising feature, a demo looks good, or someone has seen what an AI agent can do.

I understand the attraction. I like exploring technology. But when we're talking about changing how a team works, I want to start somewhere less exciting: with one real task.

## Follow the work, including the awkward bits

Pick something that happened recently. Not the ideal version in a process diagram. The actual request, with the missing field, the follow-up email and the person who knew what to do because they'd seen it before.

Then walk through it together:

- What started the task?
- What information was available, and what had to be found?
- Which systems and people did it pass through?
- Where did someone make a judgement?
- What happened when the information wasn't good enough?
- How did anyone know the task was finished?

Those questions often reveal that the most useful first improvement is a clearer handover or one less place to enter the same information.

## Consistency is a useful starting point

At Umbraco, I worked on introducing a structured process for engaging trial users through CRM automation. The point wasn't to automate a clever sequence for its own sake. It was to create more consistent touchpoints without requiring someone to remember every next step manually.

That distinction matters to me. “What can this tool do?” is an interesting question. “What do we keep forgetting, repeating or waiting for?” is often a more useful one.

## Write a small brief before you build

For a first experiment, I'd write down six things:

1. **The trigger:** what starts the work?
2. **The outcome:** what needs to be true when it's done?
3. **The inputs:** which information does it depend on?
4. **The decisions:** what can follow a rule, and what needs judgement?
5. **The owner:** who notices when it goes wrong?
6. **The baseline:** how does the task work today?

If we can't answer those yet, that isn't a failure. It's a reason to spend a little more time understanding the work before building around it.

## Make the first version smaller

I prefer one useful, understandable improvement to a grand plan that depends on everything going right. Test it on ordinary tasks and on the awkward ones. Keep a manual route available. Ask the people doing the work what has actually become easier.

The tool still matters. It just doesn't get to decide what the problem is.
