A tool can be installed, configured and available to everyone without changing much about how anyone works.

I've learned to distinguish between making a tool available and making it useful. The first is a technical milestone. The second usually involves habits, confidence and a shared understanding of why we're changing something.

## Put the tool in a real working rhythm

When I introduced Gong at Umbraco, I approached it alongside adoption frameworks, coaching routines and shared standards. The work wasn't only about getting a platform running. It was about giving it a place in how we learned and worked together.

That experience is part of why I'm interested in automation. A new capability needs to fit somewhere in an ordinary day, not just in a launch presentation.

## Make the change easy to explain

I'd want everyone involved to be able to answer three questions:

1. What problem are we trying to make smaller?
2. What should I do differently in the next real task?
3. Where do I go when it doesn't work as expected?

An explanation that relies on a product feature list probably needs another pass. Show the task, the change, and the reason it matters.

## Practise with the imperfect version

Training is more useful when it includes the actual handover, the missing information and the exception. Give people a safe way to try the process, question it and tell you what doesn't fit.

For a small first rollout, I'd use a simple rhythm:

- Walk through one ordinary task together.
- Let the team try it with support available.
- Review the points where people hesitated or used the old route.
- Adjust the process or explanation before extending it.

Going back to the old route can be useful feedback. It may mean someone needs help. It may also mean the new route is less useful than we thought.

## Look beyond logins

Usage matters, but opening a tool isn't the same as completing useful work. I'd look at whether tasks get finished, whether handovers are clearer, and whether the people doing the work say it has become easier.

Those are questions to investigate, not results I would promise in advance. The point is to keep learning after the rollout instead of treating the launch date as the finish line.
